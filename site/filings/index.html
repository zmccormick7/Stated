Filings are written for people and marked up by typesetting software. A table arrives as hundreds of nested tags, with a dollar sign in one cell, the figure in the next and the closing bracket in a third. This set is every filing read through one parser built for them, so a model or a person gets the document itself: tables with their rows and columns, headings that nest the way the filing does, and nothing left out but page numbers and running headers.

Why Markdown. A language model reads text, and Markdown is the plainest text that still keeps a document's shape: headings stay headings, and a table keeps its rows and columns. A PDF has to be turned into text before a model can read it, and tables often come apart on the way. The HTML keeps the shape but buries it in markup: Apple's 2024 annual report is 1.5 MB as filed and 0.2 MB as Markdown, so it takes about a seventh of the room in a model's context and costs far less to read.

Across companies

index.parquet has a row for every document and no text, so it can be queried where it sits. This asks what Apple had filed by a moment in time:

-- DuckDB
SELECT form, document, accepted, source
FROM 'https://data.statedlabs.com/index.parquet'
WHERE cik = '0000320193'
  AND accepted <= TIMESTAMPTZ '2020-03-31 20:00:00+00'
ORDER BY accepted;

Point in time

Every document carries accepted, the moment the SEC took the filing and made it public, in UTC. Use it, not filed, to decide what was known when: a filing accepted after 5:30 pm in New York is given the next business day as its filing date, and a results press release accepted at 4:05 pm was public before the next day's open.

The companies are the index's members month by month, not today's list. A company that was acquired or dropped out years ago is here with everything it filed, so a backtest built on this set does not quietly leave out the companies that did not survive.

How the text is parsed

We wrote a custom parser specifically for SEC filings. It reads each document's HTML and decides everything from how the filer marked it up: tags, styles, where cells sit, and the SEC's own inline XBRL tags. It does not go by what the words say. It is deterministic, so the same filing always gives the same Markdown, and each document names the parser version that wrote it.

No model rewrites anything. The words and figures are the filer's. For some tables whose markup gives no sign of which rows are headings, a small model was shown the first rows and asked how many are a title and how many are column headings. Its answer moves the heading line and nothing else.

Every document is checked, with no model involved:

  • Nothing lost. Each stretch of 60 or more letters and digits in the filing as filed must appear in the Markdown. Every document in the set passes.
  • Figures under the right period. In filings with inline XBRL, each tagged figure names its period, and a table's figures must agree about time the way the table is laid out.

Known limits

  • Some tables have no column headings. Most have none in the filing either (lists set in columns, contents pages, forms). Some have headings the markup does not set apart, and those stay as ordinary rows.
  • Images and charts are left out without a marker. Exhibits filed as scans or PDFs are not included.
  • Footnote markers are kept as text and are not linked to their footnotes.
  • Amended filings (10-K/A, 10-Q/A, 8-K/A) and exhibits other than exhibit 99 are not included.
  • Companies that file only foreign forms (20-F, 40-F, 6-K) are not covered.
